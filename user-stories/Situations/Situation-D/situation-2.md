# Situation 2 — current reality

*Third-person, what-happens only. The **same week as Situation 1**, run the way Debra actually runs it today: a paper book, a phone, and a head that holds the map. This spells everything out as if it were the only world there is.*

## The book doesn't know where she is

Debra's appointment book is paper, one page a day. It holds times; it does not hold **places**. Which office a patient belongs to, which day she's where, how long Oak Hill is from Maple Street — all of that lives in her memory. Booking is a phone call or a text: a patient asks for Tuesday 2:00, she eyeballs the page, agrees, and writes it down — and only later remembers that Tuesdays she's at Riverside and this patient always means the Eastside room. Another call to fix it.

## The back-to-back she can't reach

Last Thursday: a referral calls Tuesday — could Debra come at 10:00? The book shows 10:00 free (Harold is 9:00 to 10:00), so she writes it down. But the book holds **times, not places**: Harold is on Maple Street and the referral is across town, and the drive between them exists only in her head — where, that Tuesday, it failed to come up. She catches it the morning of, at 6 a.m., coffee in hand — visit ends 10:00, the drive is 25 minutes — and makes the day's first apology call to push the referral to 10:30. The book let her promise something physically impossible; she caught it, this time, with coffee and guilt.

## The cancellation eats the day

Thursday reads: Harold 9:00 Maple Street, Mrs. Gable 11:00 Oak Hill, Tom 2:30 south side. At 7:40 Mrs. Gable's son calls — rough night, no visit. Now the day is a morning appointment, a dead middle, and one more drive south for 2:30.

Debra wants what she always wants: the day pulled tighter. So she does it by hand. She texts Tom: *"any chance we could do this morning instead?"* — then showers, drives to Harold's, and treats him with half her mind on her phone. Tom answers at 10:15 ("sure, what time?"), by which point "10:30" is no longer possible to arrange politely; they settle on 1:00 over four more messages. She fills the gap with a grocery run she didn't plan, drives south at 12:30, and is home by 2:30 — an afternoon saved in theory, an hour of it spent negotiating by thumb from other people's driveways. Harold, at least, was never in danger of being asked: she keeps *"Harold never moves"* the same place she keeps everything — in her head.

## The cash in the glovebox

Harold pays cash at the door; it goes in the glovebox. Tom pays; glovebox. Priya paid last Tuesday — or was it the Tuesday before? The book says who she saw, not who paid. Sunday night she sits with the book, the glovebox envelope, and her texts, reconstructing the week: one patient she's nearly sure she forgot to charge, one she may have charged twice — that will be an awkward text either way. What the week actually made is an estimate.

## The rest of the business, on sticky notes

*Ask Mrs. Gable's son for a review. Flyers for the Riverside noticeboard. Order resistance bands.* These live on sticky notes on the fridge and in her phone's notes app, next to nothing, connected to nothing. The review never gets asked — Mrs. Gable rebooks by phone on a day the fridge is far away.

## The point

One week, and Debra spent it as her own dispatcher: holding the map in her head, catching a physically impossible promise at 6 a.m. because the book couldn't, rebuilding a cancelled Thursday one text at a time from a driveway, and reconstructing the week's cash from a glovebox on Sunday night. Every move Situation 1 absorbed with one question was, here, hand-made — and the one thing she most wanted from the cancellation, *a tighter day*, cost more coordination than it saved. That's current reality: a mobile practice run out of a book that has never heard of the town.
